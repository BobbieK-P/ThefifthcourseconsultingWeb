Most menus are costed once, when they're written. Then the menu goes to print, the kitchen gets busy, and the costings sit in a folder while supplier prices carry on moving.

That matters more than usual this winter. The Food and Drink Federation expects food inflation to reach around 3.9% by December and keep rising to 6.4% next summer. None of it arrives as a headline. It turns up a few pence at a time on invoices, and it quietly takes the margin off dishes that were priced months ago.

This is the second post in the **Before January** series, and it's the most practical one: a method you can do in about an hour, at a table, with a calculator.

## Why only five dishes?

In most independent venues, a handful of dishes do most of the selling. Re-costing the whole menu is a big job that tends never to get done. Re-costing your five best-sellers covers a large share of your food sales in a fraction of the time, and it tells you very quickly whether you have a problem.

## What you need

- Your till's sales report for the last four weeks, to find your five best-sellers
- Your most recent supplier invoices, ideally from this month
- The recipe and portion size for each dish. If they're in the chef's head, ask them to talk you through it
- A calculator, or a simple spreadsheet

## The method

**1. Pick your five.** Use your sales report, not your instinct. The dish everyone talks about isn't always the one that sells.

**2. List every ingredient and its portion.** Weigh it if you can. A "handful" of chips or a "ladle" of sauce is where most costings go wrong, so weigh what actually goes on the plate during a normal service, not what the recipe says.

**3. Price each ingredient from this month's invoices.** Work out the cost per gram or millilitre, then multiply by the portion. Small things add up, so don't skip the butter, oil, stock and garnish. If they're hard to cost individually, a sensible flat allowance per plate is fine.

**4. Allow for trim and waste.** Meat that's trimmed, veg that's peeled, the end of a tub that never gets used. A simple allowance of around 5% on top is better than ignoring it.

**5. Work out the GP on the price before VAT.** Divide your menu price by 1.2 to take off VAT. Then:

GP % = (price before VAT − food cost) ÷ price before VAT × 100

**6. Compare it with the GP you priced the dish on.** A slip of a point or two is normal over a few months. More than that, and the dish needs attention.

## A worked example

Take a chicken and leek pie on the menu at **£16.50**, costed at **£3.55** a portion when the menu was set in the spring. It sells around 60 a week.

At today's invoice prices, the plate looks like this:

| Ingredient | Portion | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Chicken thigh (£7.40/kg) | 180g | £1.33 |
| Puff pastry (£3.50/kg) | 120g | £0.42 |
| Leeks (£2.60/kg) | 70g | £0.18 |
| Double cream (£4.50/litre) | 60ml | £0.27 |
| Butter, stock, herbs, seasoning | allowance | £0.35 |
| Mash | 220g | £0.62 |
| Seasonal greens | 90g | £0.36 |
| Gravy | portion | £0.25 |
| **Subtotal** | | **£3.78** |
| Trim and waste allowance (5%) | | £0.19 |
| **Food cost per portion** | | **£3.97** |

The price before VAT is £16.50 ÷ 1.2 = **£13.75**.

- **In the spring:** (£13.75 − £3.55) ÷ £13.75 = **74% GP**
- **Today:** (£13.75 − £3.97) ÷ £13.75 = **71% GP**

Three points doesn't sound like much. But that's 42p less on every pie, or around £25 a week and about £1,300 a year, on one dish. Nothing about the pie has changed, and nobody in the kitchen has done anything wrong.

*(Illustrative figures, but realistic ones.)*

## What to do if the GP has slipped

You don't have to fix it all with price. There are usually three levers, and the best answer is often a mix:

- **Price.** Small, rounded increases on your strongest sellers are usually noticed least. People choose a dish they love for the dish, not the 50p.
- **Portion.** Check what's actually going on the plate. Sides are often where generosity creeps in.
- **Specification.** A different cut, supplier or garnish can bring the cost down without changing how the dish eats.

In the example, moving the pie to **£17.50** and bringing the mash back from 220g to 180g gives a food cost of around £3.85 and a GP of about **74%**, back where it started. Each pie now makes £10.73 before VAT, up from £10.20 when the menu was set.

## Make it a habit

Once you've done your five, put a date in the diary to do it again every three months, and whenever a big supplier price rise lands. Keep the costings somewhere the whole team can find them, so they don't leave with the head chef.

If your Christmas menu was priced on last year's costs, it's worth running the same check on that too.
