Autumn is when most independent operators have their heads down. Christmas menus are being finalised, party bookings are starting to come in, and the rota for December is already giving someone a headache.

It's also the point in the year when it's easiest to miss what's coming next. This winter, four things are landing on independent restaurants, pubs and bars at roughly the same time. None of them is a reason to panic. All of them are a reason to plan, and the operators who plan now will walk into January in a much stronger position than the ones who find out in February.

This is the first post in a short series called **Before January**. Every week until Christmas I'll take one of these areas and share one practical thing you can do about it.

## 1. Food costs are climbing again

After a period where food inflation had settled, it's heading back up, and it's expected to keep rising well into next year. In its September forecast, the Food and Drink Federation predicts food and soft drink inflation of around 3.9% by December 2026, peaking at 6.4% in July 2027, driven largely by energy costs and the knock-on effects of conflict in the Middle East.

For a restaurant, that doesn't arrive as a headline. It arrives as a few pence here and there on a supplier invoice, and it quietly erodes the margin on dishes that were costed months ago. A dish that cost £4.00 to make last year costs around £4.16 by Christmas and £4.26 by next summer, before anyone has changed a thing.

**What to do this month:** re-cost your five best-selling dishes using this month's invoices, not the prices you used when the menu was set. If the GP on any of them has slipped by more than a couple of points, you've found your first January priority. And if your Christmas menu was priced on last year's costs, check it before the bookings come in.

## 2. Employment law is changing, in stages

The Employment Rights Act is being introduced in phases, and several of those phases fall this autumn and winter.

From 30 October, employers must take "all reasonable steps" to prevent sexual harassment at work, and protection from harassment by third parties, such as customers, is being extended. Changes to how tips are shared, including consulting staff on your tipping policy, are now expected by the end of 2026. From 1 January 2027, the qualifying period for unfair dismissal claims drops to six months. New rules on guaranteed hours for people on zero or low-hours contracts are expected later in 2027.

These changes matter more in hospitality than in most sectors, because so much of the workforce is casual, part-time or on variable hours, and so much of the work is customer-facing.

**What to do this month:** check whether you have a written tipping policy, and whether your team has actually seen it. If you don't have one, now is the time to write it and talk it through with them. While you're at it, make sure your team knows what to do if a customer harasses them, and who to tell.

*I'm not a lawyer, and nothing here is legal advice. ACAS guidance is the best free starting point, and it's worth speaking to an HR adviser about anything specific to your business.*

## 3. Fixed costs haven't come back down

Business rates and energy are the two bills most operators have stopped questioning, simply because there's so little you can do about them day to day.

The April 2026 revaluation significantly increased rateable values for many hospitality properties, and even with the new reliefs, plenty of businesses are paying more than they were. Energy remains the sector's biggest single worry: in an ONS survey from May 2026, it ranked as the top concern for hospitality businesses.

**What to do this month:** find the end date of your energy contract and put a reminder in the diary for three months before it. Rolling onto out-of-contract rates is one of the most expensive mistakes a small venue can make, and one of the easiest to avoid.

## 4. Customers are being careful

People are still going out, but they're thinking harder about where they spend. The UK household saving ratio rose again in the spring, to 8.8%, which means more money being put aside rather than spent.

The natural reaction is to discount. But cautious customers aren't always looking for cheap. They're looking for good value, and that isn't the same thing. A well-priced set menu, a signature dish that's worth the trip, or a midweek offer built around dishes that can carry it will usually do more for you than blanket money off.

**What to do this month:** before you plan any January offers, make sure you know exactly what each dish or drink actually makes. You can't discount safely if you don't know where the margin is.
